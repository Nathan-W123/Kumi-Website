Privacy.

What KUMI holds, where it lives, who else sees it, and what you can ask us to do about it.

Last updated 27 August 2026

The short version

Right now KUMI is invitation-only, and the waitlist is the way in. If filling in that form is all you have done, we hold the email address you typed, your name and your note if you filled them in, and the fact that you came from this site. We do not forward it anywhere, and nothing on this site tracks you.

If you have an account, KUMI holds your sign-in details, everything you and your agents write inside it, and a complete copy of every repository you connect. All of it sits on one deployment we run on Railway and the database that deployment connects to, also on Railway. It is not on your machine and it is not in your own cloud, and we would rather say that plainly than let you assume otherwise.

When an agent runs, the parts of your code it reads and the instructions you gave it are sent to the model provider whose account you connected. What that provider does with them is governed by your agreement with that provider, not by this page.

We use no analytics, no advertising trackers and no third-party scripts. These marketing pages set no cookies at all. The app sets two, and both exist to keep you signed in. KUMI ships no telemetry of its own, but the vendor coding tool you connect may send its own telemetry to its own vendor, and this page says where.

You can ask for a copy of what we hold, a correction, or a deletion, by writing to kumi.support@gmail.com. Several of those we have to carry out by hand, and this page says exactly where. This page applies whether you use KUMI as an individual or on behalf of a company.

Who is responsible for this

KUMI decides what is collected and why, and is the controller of the personal data described here. It is a pre-launch product built by one person rather than an organisation with a privacy department, and the honest consequence runs through this whole page: most of what follows is done by a human answering an email, not by a button in the product. The address for all of it is kumi.support@gmail.com.

Where KUMI runs

There is one deployment, hosted on Railway. The bare clone of every repository anyone has connected, the working checkouts agents and people edit in, and any images posted into a channel sit on that deployment's own storage volume; the database is the managed Postgres it connects to, also on Railway. Your repository is a directory on that deployment's storage, alongside everyone else's, and one database holds every organisation's rows. Separation between customers is enforced in software, by roles and per-repository grants, not by separate machines or separate databases. We are not going to describe that as isolation, because it is not.

If you only joined the waitlist

The form asks three things and requires one. We store the email address, lowercased; your name and your note if you filled them in; a note of where the signup came from, which for this form is always the word "website"; the time the row was written; and, once somebody lets you through, the time that happened. Nothing else — there is no password on this row and nothing here you can sign in with.

One row per address, matched without regard to capitalisation: filling the form again updates your name and your note and leaves your place in the queue where it was. You get the same answer either way. That is deliberate — a form anybody can find that replied differently to an address it already knew would be a way to ask us which addresses those are.

Nothing is forwarded. Joining sends you no email at all, so if you are waiting to hear, nothing has gone missing. The one message this ever sends is the invitation itself, when somebody approves your row: it tells you that you are through and where to create your account.

Your IP address is used to rate-limit the form to ten submissions a minute and is held only in the running process's memory; it is never written to the database and it is gone when the process restarts.

Being honest about the gap: there is a way to delete a waitlist row, but it is ours, not yours — an operator can remove one, and there is no unsubscribe link and no way for you to do it yourself. Write to kumi.support@gmail.com and we will remove it. The same goes for seeing what your row holds: there is no page that shows it to you, so ask and we will tell you.

Your account

An account holds your email address, which is also how you sign in and is unique regardless of capitalisation; the display name your colleagues see; your password as a scrypt hash, never as text we could read; whether the account is disabled or is an administrator; when it was created; and the two accent colours and agent colour you pick. Those colours are deliberately visible to your colleagues, so people can tell whose agents are whose.

There is no profile photo on our servers. The product has no field for one. If you choose a picture, the browser keeps it locally and never uploads it; everywhere else, your initials stand in.

Signing in, and what that records

Each browser session stores a hashed session secret, a hashed CSRF token, when it was created, when it expires, when it was last seen, your IP address, and the first 512 characters of your browser's user-agent string. Sessions last twelve hours and do not extend themselves. Signing out deletes the row.

A session you never sign out of is a different story. The code that would sweep expired sessions exists but nothing calls it, so the IP address and user-agent on an expired session stay in the database until we remove them by hand. We would rather write that down than claim a retention limit we do not enforce.

API tokens store the name you gave them, a hash of the secret, their scopes, and the IP address and time they were last used. Revoking one marks it revoked and keeps the row on purpose, so the record that the credential existed survives. Those rows are not swept either.

Failed sign-ins are counted in memory against the address that was typed; ten failures lock that address for fifteen minutes, and the count is cleared by a successful sign-in or a restart. Password-reset records hold the address and a hashed token, and are deleted when the reset is used or another is requested. Invitation records hold the invited person's email address, the role offered and who invited them; revoking an invitation stamps it as revoked rather than deleting it, so an address invited once stays until we remove it by hand, including for someone who never accepted.

What you and your team write

KUMI stores channel messages and their replies, reactions, direct messages between two people including whether and when they were read, review comments on a changeset, approval decisions and the comments left with them, the objective you typed when you asked for work, and the transcript of the thread that request was made inside, which is copied onto the task as context. Pins and deletions record who did them.

You can delete your own reply or your own direct message. A channel message that already has replies is blanked in place and keeps a record of who deleted it, so the thread still makes sense. Deleting a repository removes its whole channel. There is nothing in the product that removes everything one person has written across a whole account.

Your repositories

Connecting a repository takes a full bare clone of it — every file, every branch, the entire history — onto the deployment. From that, KUMI creates working checkouts: one per running task, one per integration attempt, one per planning session, a long-lived one per person for editing in the browser, and one that runs a preview of your app. The database additionally holds the diff of every file an agent changed, stored verbatim as the patch text itself, along with the agent's explanation, the commands it ran, the test results, and the symbols and dependencies it touched. Your source code is therefore in the database as well as on the disk.

Deleting a repository deletes the clone from disk and cascades across most of its rows. Two things it does not reach: the per-person editing checkouts, and any images posted into that repository's channel. Working copies of code can therefore outlive the repository they came from until we remove them by hand.

Images posted into a channel

Images are stored as files on the deployment's own storage volume, alongside your repositories, up to eight megabytes each. There is no code that deletes one. Deleting the message that showed an image does not remove the file. Screenshots tend to contain whatever was on the screen at the time, so this is worth knowing before you paste one.

What is kept from an agent run

Not the raw exchange with the model. KUMI itself writes no transcript of it: the vendor tool's streamed output is held in memory while the run happens and is discarded when the process ends, and the temporary home the tool signs in from — including any session file the tool wrote there — is deleted at the end of the run. There is one exception, for Codex, described next. What is kept is the agent's own narration, posted into the channel and recorded in the audit log; its plan; its explanation of what it changed; and token counts.

That exception, stated because we would rather you read it here than find it. For Codex specifically, KUMI reads the last 64 kilobytes of the newest Codex session file before deleting that temporary home, in order to recover rate-limit figures, and writes that fragment to a plain unencrypted file — one per person. What is stored is the raw tail of that session file, not just the figures. It is overwritten by the next Codex run and nothing else deletes it. This should store only the numbers it is actually after, and it is on the list to fix.

The audit log

KUMI keeps an append-only, hash-chained record of what happened: who signed in, who was invited or removed, who issued or revoked a token, who submitted or cancelled a task, who approved what, and which messages were posted, edited or deleted. It stores internal user ids rather than names, and it does not store the text of channel messages — only the message's id. It does store the email address of anyone invited to an organisation, the objective you typed when submitting a task in full, the comment left with an approval decision, and the agent's own narration of a run.

Anyone with view access to a project can read that project's audit timeline: who ran what, task objectives in full, and approval comments. Invitation records, which carry the invited email address, are written to the same log but are scoped to the organisation and are not returned by the project timeline.

The log is built to resist being edited, and that cuts against you as well as for you. Database triggers refuse every update outright, and refuse a deletion unless an operator checkpoint already covers the rows. That is the property that makes the log worth having, and it is also why we cannot simply wipe this one store when you ask us to. The retention section below says what that means for you.

Server logs

There is no HTTP access log: no request line, path, IP address or user-agent is written per request. What does reach the deployment's log is operational diagnostics carrying internal identifiers, an account email address if a trial-ending notice fails to send, a user id if a password-reset message fails to send, and the first eighty characters of a channel message that no agent picked up.

One more, stated because it matters: a deployment with no mail relay configured writes the whole outgoing message into its own log instead of sending it, and password-reset messages contain a working reset link. Logs go to the hosting platform's log system. The product sets no rotation and no retention period, so how long they are held is Railway's setting rather than ours.

Billing

KUMI is not generally available and tiered billing is being reworked. Where a paid sign-up or an upgrade does run, your email address and an internal organisation identifier are sent to Stripe, and we store the Stripe customer and subscription ids, the subscription status, the trial end and the period end. Card details are handled by Stripe's own hosted checkout and never reach KUMI — there is no field for a card anywhere in our database. On a deployment with no Stripe key configured, nothing reaches Stripe at all.

Why we hold it, and on what basis

To provide the product you asked for: performance of our contract with you. That covers your account, the content you and your team create, the repositories you connect, and the agent runs you start.

To keep the product secure and working: our legitimate interests. That covers the IP address and user-agent on a session, the last-used IP on an API token, rate limiting, and the audit log. We think a small team needs to be able to see who did what to a shared codebase, and that this outweighs the modest intrusion of recording it. If you disagree in your own case, you can object — see the rights section.

Because you asked to hear from us: consent, for the waitlist. You can withdraw it at any time by writing to us.

To take payment and keep the records that go with it: contract and, for records we are required to retain, legal obligation.

We do not use your content, your messages or your repositories to train a model of our own. KUMI has no model of its own. Two small models do run locally inside the deployment, to filter channel chatter and to write short summaries; they are fixed, they do not learn from what they read, and nothing they read leaves the process.

Model providers, and why this is the important one

KUMI never calls a model API itself. It runs the vendor's own command-line tool on the deployment, with a checkout of your repository beside it, and that tool sends what it reads to its own vendor. So the contents of the files the agent opens, the diffs it produces, the objective you typed and the surrounding thread messages go to the provider you connected. The product can connect Anthropic (Claude Code), OpenAI (Codex), Cursor and Amazon (Kiro) today; support for Google and GitHub Copilot exists in the code but is not offered. Which one receives your code depends entirely on which account you connected.

Because it is your account, that provider's terms and your own plan with them decide how long they keep it and whether any of it is used to improve their models. We cannot see that, we cannot change it, and we cannot delete anything on your behalf. If it matters — and for source code it usually does — read the terms of the account before you connect it.

One related thing, so that "no telemetry" is not overstated anywhere on this site: the vendor tool you connect may send its own telemetry to its own vendor. KUMI's network allowlist for the Claude Code tool deliberately permits Anthropic's telemetry endpoint, which is a host that tool reaches on its own. That is the tool's telemetry, not ours. KUMI ships none of its own and sends nothing to it.

Who else receives data

Railway hosts the deployment. Everything described on this page — the database, your repositories, the working checkouts, the images, the logs — sits on infrastructure Railway operates.

Stripe receives your email address and an internal organisation identifier when a checkout runs, and handles card details directly. Nothing reaches Stripe on a deployment with no Stripe key configured.

GitHub receives what you ask it to. If you connect an account, KUMI obtains a token on your behalf through GitHub's device flow, reads your GitHub login name back so it can be displayed, clones the repositories you choose over HTTPS, and pushes completed work back only when you ask for it.

The model providers receive your code and your prompts, as described above.

An email relay receives the recipient's address and the body of the messages KUMI sends. There are three today: a sign-up link, a password reset, and a notice that a trial is ending. A fourth, an account confirmation code, is in the code and is off unless the deployment turns it on. Which relay is a setting on the deployment rather than something the product names, so we are not naming one here that might be wrong. Ask and we will tell you which one this deployment uses.

Hugging Face is listed for completeness. Two small models come from it. The one that filters channel chatter is downloaded when the container is built. The summariser is not: it is downloaded the first time this deployment needs it, so Hugging Face sees a download request from our server and nothing else. No data about you or your code is ever sent to it.

We do not sell your personal information and we do not share it for advertising.

How long we keep it

Our rule is thirty days after you ask us to delete your account, or after we terminate it, and then it is purged.

Now the honest part. There is no account-deletion feature in the product today. No route, no button, and no code anywhere that removes a user record or an organisation. The strongest thing an administrator can do is disable an account, which blocks sign-in and revokes live sessions and leaves everything else in place. So when you ask us to delete, we do it by hand against the database, on that same thirty-day rule. We are telling you this because a policy that describes a purge the software does not perform is worth nothing.

Several things do not age out at all today, and you should know which: expired sessions with their IP address and user-agent; revoked and expired API tokens with their last-used IP; invitation records holding an invited address; waitlist rows; registered worker records; token-usage accounting; images posted into channels; and the per-person editing checkouts of a repository. For sessions and API tokens the sweep code is written but nothing calls it, so those rows are simply never removed. Nothing in the product removes any of this on a schedule. Removing it is a manual job a person does, on request or when we clean up.

The audit log is the hardest case, and the one place where we will not promise complete erasure. It is deliberately built so that individual entries cannot be removed or altered without the whole record showing it. The database itself refuses: one trigger rejects any update, another rejects any deletion the operator has not first checkpointed. What we can do is retire a whole segment of it once an operator checkpoint covers that segment, and that is what we do when we purge an account. Until then, what remains is your internal user id, the record of actions taken, task objectives, approval comments, the agent's narration of a run, and the email address of anyone invited.

Stripe keeps its own billing records under its own retention rules. KUMI has no way to delete a customer at Stripe, so a purge here does not reach them.

Your rights

You can ask for a copy of the personal data we hold about you, ask us to correct it, ask us to delete it, ask for it in a portable form, object to our processing it on the basis of our legitimate interests, ask us to restrict processing while a question is open, and withdraw consent for the waitlist.

Write to kumi.support@gmail.com from the address on your account, or from the address you gave the waitlist. We aim to answer within thirty days and will tell you if we need longer and why.

There is no export button and no delete button in the product. Putting together everything held about one person means querying more than twenty tables and several places on the deployment's disk — your repository clones, the working checkouts, your editing overlay, the images, and the credential store — by hand. We would rather describe that plainly than imply a self-serve process that does not exist. If you are in the UK or the EU and think we have handled this badly, you can also complain to your local data protection authority.

If you are in California

The categories of personal information KUMI collects are: identifiers, meaning your name, email address, IP address and internal account id; commercial information, meaning subscription status where billing runs; internet and network activity, meaning session records, read markers and last-seen timestamps; professional information, meaning the company, team size and agents you may have told us about; and the content you give us, meaning your messages, your task descriptions, your repositories, and the diffs your agents produce from them.

We hold two kinds of sensitive personal information. The first is your sign-in credentials — your email address together with the hash of your password. The second, if you connect one, is the credential for another account: the token or key your coding agent signs in with, and a GitHub token. Both are held to authenticate, and the connected credentials are stored encrypted and are never returned to a browser. We never use either, or any other sensitive personal information, to infer characteristics about you, so there is nothing for a request to limit their use to act on.

We collect this from you, from your browser, and from the agent runs you start. We use it for the purposes set out above. We disclose it to the service providers named above for those purposes. We have not sold personal information and we have not shared it for cross-context behavioural advertising in the preceding twelve months, and we do not intend to start.

California residents have the right to know what is collected and disclosed, to delete it, to correct it, to opt out of its sale or sharing, to limit the use of sensitive personal information, and not to be treated worse for exercising any of those. Exercise any of them by writing to kumi.support@gmail.com. An authorised agent may make a request on your behalf, and we will ask for proof that you authorised them.

Being straight about whether this applies: the CCPA and CPRA bind businesses that meet at least one of three thresholds — annual gross revenue above twenty-five million dollars, handling the personal information of a hundred thousand or more California consumers or households, or drawing half or more of revenue from selling or sharing personal information. KUMI meets none of them, and will not for a long time. We are setting these rights out anyway, because we would rather work this way from the start than switch it on the day we have to.

International transfers

KUMI runs on one Railway deployment. Stripe, GitHub and the model providers are United States companies, so using KUMI means your personal data reaches the United States. The deployment's own region is a setting rather than something published here — ask us and we will tell you where it is.

We are not going to claim a specific transfer mechanism on this page that we cannot show you. If having one in place is a condition of you using KUMI, write to kumi.support@gmail.com before you sign up and we will tell you honestly where it stands.

Children

KUMI is a tool for professional software teams and is not directed at children. There is no age field anywhere in the product and nothing checks one, so we are relying on you here. We do not knowingly collect personal information from anyone under sixteen. If you believe a child has given us information, write to kumi.support@gmail.com and we will remove it.

Cookies

The app sets two cookies, both at the moment a session begins — when you sign in, finish signing up, accept an invitation, or complete a password reset — both scoped to the whole site, both marked SameSite=Strict, and both expiring with the twelve-hour session. One holds the reference to your session and is not readable by scripts. The other holds a token the page reads and echoes back, which is how KUMI knows a request came from a page you were actually on. Signing out clears both.

That is every cookie the product sets. There is no analytics cookie, no advertising cookie, and no third-party cookie. These marketing pages set none at all, which is why there is no consent banner on them: there is nothing to consent to.

What your browser keeps by itself

The dashboard remembers some things locally, in your browser, which are never uploaded to us: a profile picture if you chose one, a picture per channel, unsent message drafts, which notifications you have read and how far, your last organisation, project, repository and agent, your favourites and muted channels, remembered image sizes, and appearance settings such as theme, accent colour, layout and panel widths, diff mode and whether message sounds are on.

Signing out does not clear any of it. It is cleared only when a different account signs in on the same browser, and even then several things are deliberately kept, because they describe the browser rather than the person: your channel pictures, remembered image sizes, and the appearance settings — theme, accent colour, layout and panel widths, diff mode, and whether message sounds are on. On a shared or borrowed machine, clear the site's data yourself. Nothing we do on our side can reach into your browser, so deleting your account does not touch this.

The desktop app stores the server address and your access token in a file in your operating system's user-data directory, with the token sealed by the operating system's own keystore. Signing out removes the token from that file.

No analytics, no advertising

KUMI ships no analytics package, no tag manager, no tracking pixel, no session recorder, no error-reporting service and no advertising code, in the product or on this site. Every font, script and image on the page you are reading is served from this origin: no Google Fonts, no CDN, no embeds. The pages carry a Content-Security-Policy that allows scripts only from this origin, so a third-party tracker could not run here even if one were added by mistake.

The one qualification is the one made above: the vendor coding tool you connect may send its own telemetry to its own vendor, and KUMI's network allowlist for Claude Code deliberately permits Anthropic's telemetry endpoint. That traffic is the tool's, not ours. KUMI ships none of its own.

How this is protected, and what we do not claim

Passwords are stored as scrypt hashes. Session secrets, CSRF tokens, API tokens, reset tokens and invitation secrets are stored only as SHA-256 digests, so the values themselves are not in the database. The credentials you connect for a coding agent, and a GitHub token if you connect one, are encrypted with AES-256-GCM on the deployment. Access is controlled by organisation roles and per-repository grants. The audit log is hash-chained. Sign-in and the waitlist are rate limited.

What we are not claiming: KUMI holds no SOC 2 report, no ISO 27001 certification and nothing HIPAA-related, and it has not been through an external security audit. Repositories, working checkouts, images and the database itself are stored plainly — any encryption at rest is a property of the hosting platform, not something the product does. Agent commands can be run inside a Docker sandbox with a restricted network, and that sandbox is optional rather than on by default. If any of this needs to be otherwise before you could use KUMI, ask us and we will tell you where it actually stands.
